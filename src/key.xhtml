How to read this book

This e-book reproduces the printed chos spyod of Ka-Nying Shedrub Ling, the pecha, as faithfully as a screen allows. Every word of the printed text is here, in the printed order, at the printed sizes. Nothing has been re-edited.

A printed book can be flicked through, held open at two places, and marked with slips of paper. A screen cannot, so a small set of reading and navigation aids has been added. They are the only additions, and each one is set apart typographically so that there is never any doubt about what is printed in the chos spyod and what the editor supplied.

What is printed

སྐྱབས་སུ་མཆིའོ། །
Text to recite. The large letters of the pecha, in the eBook reader's own ink. By eBook reader this page means the app the book is open in, such as Apple Books, Google Play Books or Kobo, not the person reading.
ཞེས་ལན་གསུམ།
Small letters, yig chung. Instructions, colophons and notes, printed small in the pecha and set small here, one degree quieter, hanging from the same head line as the large letters.

What the editor added

Two colours carry the whole system. Red marks what the editor supplied: landing points, repeat braces, and refrains written out where the print abbreviates. Blue, the eBook reader's own link colour, marks what can be tapped. Black is the pecha.

ལ་སོགས་པ།ལ་སོགས་པ།
Landing point. A red jewel in braces marks a place that links elsewhere bring you to, usually the first line to recite after a jump. With a short label when several sit close together:
ལ་སོགས་པ།མཆོད་པ།ལ་སོགས་པ།
ལ་སོགས་པ། ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 ལ་སོགས་པ།
Jump. A blue link in braces leads to the text that follows in actual recitation. The label is the opening words or title of the destination, the number its page in the printed book, and the triangle points forward or back through the book:
ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 back   ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 forward
ཐུགས་སྒྲུབ་ཟུར་འདེབས། 340
Jump to another text. Two triangles mean the jump leaves the prayer you are in for a different one; one triangle stays within it.
ཨོཾ་མ་ཎི་པདྨེ་ཧཱུྃ་ཧྲཱིཿ
ཨོཾ་མུ་ནི་མུ་ནི་མ་ཧཱ་མུ་ན་ཡེ་སྭཱཧཱ།
Repeat. Red braces enclose a passage to be repeated; a number after the closing brace says how many times, none means as many as the practice requires. The closing brace is itself a link back to the opening one, for long passages.
ལ་སོགས་པ༴ཕྱག་འཚལ་ལོ།
Written-out refrain. Where the pecha abbreviates a repeated line with the mark ༴, the editor has written the line out after it in red, so it can be read without turning back. The black text and the mark are printed; the red is not.
ལ་སོགས་པ།࿙དམིགས་ཡུལ།ལ་སོགས་པ།
Annotation. A red dotted curve, the mchan rtags with which a pecha's marginal notes are hung on a word, followed by the editor's note in its shortest form: a variant reading, an added syllable. Tapping it opens the Annotations page, where the note is given in full with a jump back.
༼ ཞལ་འདོན། ▸༽
To the recitation. Where a prayer opens with a long passage in small letters, an instruction, a narrative or a colophon, this link skips past it to the first line to recite. The seven-chapter prayer has one at the head of each chapter.
Heading, page number, arrows
Titles. Every prayer carries a red title with the page on which it starts in the printed book and arrows to the previous and next prayer. The titles are those of the printed table of contents. Sections within one practice have smaller, quieter titles with small triangles flush to the name.
Page numbers
Printed pages. Page breaks that fall mid-sentence in the pecha are not shown; the page number of each prayer stands above its title, and the དཀར་ཆག at the front lists them all.

The jumps and the order they suggest come from the editor's own experience of practice at Ka-Nying Shedrub Ling and at Gomde centres around the world. They are admittedly incomplete, and somewhat arbitrary: prayers are recited in different orders at different places and on different occasions. If there are jumps or guidance you would find useful in a coming version, please reach out to the editor.

Typography

The Tibetan is set in Monlam Uni OuChan2, an uchen face from the Monlam IT Research Centre, embedded in the book so it reads the same on every device. It was chosen for how it reads on a screen at body size; its vowel signs, anusvāra and visarga are positioned by the font itself.

Tibetan hangs from a head line rather than sitting on a baseline. Where small letters share a line with large ones they are raised so that the head lines meet, as they do on the printed page. All added marks, the braces ༼ ༽, the jewel ࿉, the abbreviation mark ༴ and the numerals, are letters of the Tibetan Unicode block drawn from the same font, not symbols borrowed from elsewhere, so they match the text in weight and colour. The only exceptions are the small triangles, which are arrows and not Tibetan.

Colour follows the convention above wherever the eBook reader allows. In a dark theme some eBook readers repaint all text in their own ink; the braces, jewels and title rules keep their colour, the words do not.
