Notes from the Editor

This e-book reproduces the printed chos spyod of Ka-Nying Shedrub Ling, the pecha, as faithfully as a screen allows. Every word of the printed text is here, in the printed order, at the printed sizes. Nothing has been re-edited.

A printed book can be flicked through, held open at two places, and marked with slips of paper. A screen cannot, so a small set of reading and navigation aids has been added. They are the only additions, and each one is set apart typographically so that there is never any doubt about what is printed in the chos spyod and what the editor supplied.

What is printed

སྐྱབས་སུ་མཆིའོ། ། Text to recite. The large letters of the pecha, in the reader's own ink.
ཞེས་ལན་གསུམ། Small letters, yig chung. Instructions, colophons and notes, printed small in the pecha and set small here, one degree quieter, hanging from the same head line as the large letters.

What the editor added

Two colours carry the whole system. Red marks what the editor supplied: landing points, repeat braces, and refrains written out where the print abbreviates. Blue, the reader's own link colour, marks what can be tapped. Black is the pecha.

ལ་སོགས་པ།ལ་སོགས་པ། Landing point. A red jewel in braces marks a place that links elsewhere bring you to, usually the first line to recite after a jump. With a short label when several sit close together:
ལ་སོགས་པ།མཆོད་པ།ལ་སོགས་པ།
ལ་སོགས་པ། ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 ལ་སོགས་པ། Jump. A blue link in braces leads to the text that follows in actual recitation. The label is the opening words or title of the destination, the number its page in the printed book, and the triangle points forward or back through the book:
ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 back   ཨོཾ་རྗེ་བཙུན་མ། 114 forward
ཐུགས་སྒྲུབ་ཟུར་འདེབས། 340 Jump to another text. Two triangles mean the jump leaves the prayer you are in for a different one; one triangle stays within it.
ཨོཾ་མ་ཎི་པདྨེ་ཧཱུྃ་ཧྲཱིཿ
ཨོཾ་མུ་ནི་མུ་ནི་མ་ཧཱ་མུ་ན་ཡེ་སྭཱཧཱ།		 Repeat. Red braces enclose a passage to be repeated; a number after the closing brace says how many times, none means as many as the practice requires. The closing brace is itself a link back to the opening one, for long passages.
ལ་སོགས་པ༴ཕྱག་འཚལ་ལོ། Written-out refrain. Where the pecha abbreviates a repeated line with the mark ༴, the editor has written the line out after it in red, so it can be read without turning back. The black text and the mark are printed; the red is not.
༼ ཞལ་འདོན། ▸༽ To the recitation. In the seven-chapter prayer each chapter opens with narrative in small letters; this link skips to its first line to recite.
Heading, page number, arrows Titles. Every prayer carries a red title with the page on which it starts in the printed book and arrows to the previous and next prayer. The titles are those of the printed table of contents. Sections within one practice have smaller, quieter titles with small triangles flush to the name.
Page numbers Printed pages. Page breaks that fall mid-sentence in the pecha are not shown; the page number of each prayer stands under its title, and the table of contents at the end lists them all.

Reading preferences

Small letters, yig chung: reader's ink སྐྱབས་སུ་མཆིའོ། །ཞེས་ལན་གསུམ།

Tap the blue word to switch between the reader's ink and green ink. The choice is kept for the whole book, on this device, until you change it here. In a dark theme some readers repaint all text in their own ink and the green will not show.

Typography

The Tibetan is set in Noto Serif Tibetan, an uchen face released under the SIL Open Font License and embedded in the book, so it reads the same on Apple and Android. It was chosen for correctness rather than looks: its vowel signs, anusvāra and visarga are positioned by the font itself on every letter, which older Tibetan fonts leave to the reading system and often get wrong.

Tibetan hangs from a head line rather than sitting on a baseline. Where small letters share a line with large ones they are raised so that the head lines meet, as they do on the printed page. All added marks, the braces ༼ ༽, the jewel ࿉, the abbreviation mark ༴ and the numerals, are letters of the Tibetan Unicode block drawn from the same font, not symbols borrowed from elsewhere, so they match the text in weight and colour. The only exceptions are the small triangles, which are arrows and not Tibetan.

Colour follows the convention above wherever the reading system allows. In a dark theme some readers repaint all text in their own ink; the braces, jewels and title rules keep their colour, the words do not.
